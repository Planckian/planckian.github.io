Zero-Overhead Global Control · Ring Routing

The Global-Control Ring Four control channels. No auxiliary qubits. Everything you want to do, you do by moving qubits with global pulses until they arrive where the local control lives.

The machine, and what you do with it

A closed ring of N qubits with XY nearest-neighbour couplings. Every circle is a computational qubit — there are no ancillas, no buffer qubits, no second species.

The whole control budget Two global couplings — g(t) on the odd bonds, h(t) on the even bonds — plus a single locally addressable site ī with its two green bonds and its own drive. Four channels. Grow the ring to 12, 16, 26 qubits and it is still four.
  1. Fire a global layer. One click applies the gate to every bond of that colour simultaneously. You cannot touch one bond of a global layer without touching all of them.
  2. Each layer is one SWAP layer. The XY interaction natively gives iSWAP at Jτ = 3π/2; a true SWAP costs 3 iSWAP + 3 √X global pulses. The counter tracks both.
  3. Everything is routing. Local gates exist only at ī. To act on a qubit you must transport it there — the two global layers are your only engine.
  4. Four missions. Transport one qubit, entangle a chosen pair, do the same in the keyhole geometry, then read out a code's syndromes. Each exposes a different property of the architecture.
  5. Then read the theory. The last stage explains the routing group, the pair-routing obstruction you will hit in stage 3, and why any of this makes error correction cheap.

Why this works

Everything you just did is a statement about one permutation group.

The two global layers are the alternating transposition products π_g = (0 1)(2 3)···(N−2 N−1) π_h = (1 2)(3 4)···(N−1 0) π_L = π_g ∘ π_h , π_R = π_L⁻¹

Transport: why one qubit can always get there

Under π_L the two sublattices rigidly counter-rotate: an even site's content goes to e − 2k, an odd site's to o + 2k. So π_L generates the full cyclic translation group of each sublattice — any two sites of equal parity are joined by some π_L^k, and one extra π_g (or π_h) fixes a parity mismatch. Every qubit can reach ī.

The cost is min{d, N/2 − d} composite steps with d = ½(t − p) mod N/2 — at worst O(N) global pulses, and crucially independent of which qubit you addressed. That is the whole trick: locality of control is traded for depth of routing, and the number of control lines never grows.

Entanglement: the obstruction you hit

Pair routing is strictly harder, and stage 3 shows why. The layers translate the two sublattices rigidly, so the separation of an even–odd pair shifts by a fixed amount at every step — after k steps the gap is q − p + 4k (mod N). Only gaps congruent mod gcd(4,N) are ever reachable. The pairs the global dynamics alone can bring into the local region therefore form a single cycle through all N qubits: connected — hence still universal by composition — but every vertex has degree two, so a gate between distant qubits compiles into a chain.

The fix is to use the two green links at ī as transport primitives as well. They are single transpositions that do not respect the rigid counter-rotation, and they break the congruence.

Keyhole: buying all-to-all

Attach two ports through locally controlled links, π_a = (0 a) and π_b = (1 b). The ports are inert under the global layers, so these swaps commute with the ring motion: park the first qubit at a, then route the second to site 1 without disturbing it, and swap it to b. Any pair, always. A short locally controlled corridor lifts the effective two-qubit connectivity to all-to-all — the price of a little local control.

Why iSWAP everywhere, and why it is fine

The XY coupling at Jτ = 3π/2 gives iSWAP, not SWAP. Transport needs a true exchange, so each SWAP layer is SWAP = (I⊗√X) iSWAP (√X⊗I) iSWAP (I⊗√X) iSWAP — three global entangling pulses and three global single-qubit pulses. With a coupler that also mediates ZZ it collapses to SWAP = (S†⊗S†) iSWAP CZ. Either way the count is fixed by the graph, not by the site.

The payoff: error correction with zero overhead

Because every physical qubit is computational, one code protects the whole register — no second, interleaved correction cycle for ancillas, no redundancy devoted to the addressing infrastructure. A cyclic [[n,1,d]] code with n = 4d − 7 matches the ring exactly: its stabilizer extraction is nothing but identical two-qubit gates on disjoint alternating bonds, which is the global dynamics. Syndrome qubits rotate around the ring accumulating stabilizer information in O(n) native operations.

Only readout is serial, which is what stage 4 made you feel: with ℓ measurement sites you need M = ⌈n_s/ℓ⌉ rounds for n_s = n − 1 = 4d − 8 syndromes. That single number is the wiring/performance dial — more local read-out, shallower syndrome extraction, better threshold.

Monte Carlo on this architecture puts the threshold near 10⁻³, roughly seven orders of magnitude above earlier estimates for globally-controlled arrays that had to protect their auxiliary qubits separately.

Reading the picture

Red bonds carry the global coupling g(t), blue bonds h(t); firing one applies the exchange on all of its bonds at once. Green bonds are the two locally controlled links flanking the dashed site ī, the only place single-qubit gates and native two-qubit gates live. Numbered discs are the qubit states, which move; the sites themselves do not.
