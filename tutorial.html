A quantum computer that runs on four wires

Every game at this booth is one step of a single argument: that you can drive an entire quantum processor with a handful of shared fields, and that doing so costs you depth rather than hardware. This page is that argument, start to finish, with the games attached where they belong.

No prior quantum computing needed for sections 1, 3 and 5. Sections 2 and 4 have matrices in them, and you can skip them without losing the thread.

six minutes · companion to arXiv:2608.05821

01

The problem is not the qubits. It's the wires.

A superconducting qubit needs several dedicated lines: something to drive it, something to tune it, something to read it out. That is fine for fifty qubits. At a million — the scale fault tolerance actually demands — it is a catastrophe. Every line carries heat into a dilution refrigerator, occupies space on a chip edge, needs its own calibration, and constrains the layout of everything around it. The cost grows faster than the qubit count. This is the wiring problem, and it is arguably a harder obstacle to scale than coherence.

Two ways to control a chip. On the left, every qubit owns its wires. On the right, two global couplings drive all the bonds at once, one site is individually addressable, and the count does not change when you grow the ring.

Global control is the obvious escape: drive the whole register with a few shared fields. The catch, historically, is that you still need some way to single out a qubit, and every previous proposal paid for that with auxiliary qubits — buffers, pointers, marker atoms — woven through the register. Those helpers store no information, so the error-correcting code protecting your data does not protect them. Fixing that meant running two interleaved correction cycles, one for data and one for the helpers, each with its own redundancy. The overhead swallowed the idea.

The architecture behind these games removes the helpers entirely. Every physical qubit is a computational qubit, so one code covers the whole device, and the symmetry breaking that makes the machine universal comes from a single locally driven site.

The gate you actually have is not the gate you want

iSWAP = SWAP · diag(1, i, i, 1). The leftover diagonal looks innocent. It is not: diag(1,i,i,1) = e^{iπ/4} e^{−iπ/4 Z⊗Z}, which is a CZ up to single-qubit rotations — an entangling gate. You cannot push it onto one qubit and track it in software.

If the partner site happened to be empty, that leftover would reduce to a harmless phase on the qubit you moved. In a real register the partner holds data, and then every hop deposits a genuine two-qubit phase. Transport through a populated machine needs the real exchange. So you build it:

SWAP = (I⊗√X) iSWAP (√X⊗I) iSWAP (I⊗√X) iSWAP A matrix product acts right to left, so the rightmost factor is the first pulse fired. Three entangling pulses and three single-qubit ones — and an exhaustive search finds nothing shorter, plus exactly four words of that length.

Why three and not two? Watch the operator while you build it. SWAP has four non-zero entries and so does iSWAP; the first √X sends it dense, and the later iSWAPs are what let those amplitudes interfere back down to four. You have to go out into the dense region, turn around, and come back — and each leg costs an entangling pulse.

03

Now make every pulse global

In the architecture nothing addresses a single bond. The couplings come in two flavours, α on one set of bonds and β on the other, and firing one applies the gate to all of its bonds simultaneously. The √X drives are global too — each addresses a whole sublattice, the even-indexed qubits or the odd-indexed ones.

That is exactly the structure the compilation needs. Inside a bond, one qubit is even and the other odd, so √X⊗I and I⊗√X become "drive the even sublattice" and "drive the odd sublattice". Fire the six-pulse sequence with global pulses and you have performed a SWAP on every bond of that colour at once.

Two layers, alternating. Violet rings are the even sublattice, amber the odd — the two targets of the global √X drives. Each layer is a set of disjoint pairs, so the six-pulse compilation runs on all of them in parallel.

On an open chain this has a visible flaw. The α layer pairs everyone up, but the β layer leaves the two end qubits without partners — and a global drive cannot exempt them. They collect stray rotations and light up with excitation that was never yours. Close the chain into a ring and both layers become perfect matchings, every qubit sits inside some SWAP block at every step, and the stray rotation has nowhere to land. That is why the architecture is a ring, and it is not a stylistic choice.

04

Routing is the computation

Once each layer is a genuine SWAP, firing layers permutes the register. Label the ring sites and the two layers become products of transpositions:

π_g = (0 1)(2 3)···(N−2 N−1) π_h = (1 2)(3 4)···(N−1 0) π_L = π_g ∘ π_h even sites → e−2 , odd sites → o+2 Fire g then h and the two sublattices rigidly counter-rotate, one step each way per application.

Because π_L translates each sublattice cyclically, any qubit can be walked to any site: match the parity with one extra layer if needed, then apply π_L the right number of times. The cost is min{d, N/2−d} composite steps — O(N) pulses at worst, and crucially independent of which qubit you addressed. That is the whole bargain of global control: you give up local addressing and you pay in circuit depth, not in hardware.

Universality then follows from almost nothing. One site of the ring has its own drive and its own two couplings. Route any qubit there for an arbitrary single-qubit rotation; route any pair onto the local link for an entangling gate. Arbitrary single-qubit gates plus iSWAP is a universal set, so the machine is universal.

The catch

The layers move the sublattices rigidly, so the gap between an even and an odd qubit only ever changes by multiples of four. Most pairs can never be brought together by global pulses alone.

The fix

Use the two local links as transport too — single transpositions that break the rigidity. Or hang two ports off the ring: being inert under the global layers, they commute with the motion, and the connectivity becomes all-to-all.

05

So how many control lines does a chip need?

Step back from the ring. Take any lattice of qubits with any pattern of couplings, and ask the general question: how few global lines can drive it? One line fires a set of bonds simultaneously, and simultaneous gates must act on disjoint pairs — so each line's bonds must form a matching. The question becomes: into how few matchings can the bonds be split?

Splitting a lattice into matchings. Each colour class is a set of bonds that share no qubit, so all of them can fire on one pulse. The fourth game makes you find such a splitting by hand.

Necessary

Take the busiest qubit, the one touching Δ bonds. All Δ meet at the same site, so no two can share a line. You need at least Δ.

Sufficient

For a bipartite lattice König's theorem splits the bonds into exactly Δ matchings — never more. Drop bipartiteness and Vizing gives you Δ or Δ+1.

So the answer is the coordination number: the number of global control lines a lattice needs is exactly the largest number of neighbours any one qubit has. And notice what the argument never used — which gate the line performs. It only used disjointness. Gates on disjoint pairs commute whatever they are, so a line running SWAP and a line running iSWAP cost exactly the same. Fix your connectivity and you have fixed your control budget.

06

Why all of this makes error correction cheap

Here is where the zero-overhead claim pays off. Because no qubit is reserved for addressing, there is no second population to protect and no second correction cycle to interleave — one code covers the entire device.

The code that fits is a cyclic stabilizer code [[n,1,d]] with n = 4d−7: qubit count linear in the distance, rather than quadratic as for planar codes. Its stabilizer generators are cyclic shifts of one another, which means its syndrome extraction circuit consists entirely of identical two-qubit gates applied to disjoint alternating pairs — which is the global dynamics of the ring. Syndrome qubits simply rotate around the ring, picking up stabilizer information as they go.

Only one thing cannot be done globally: measurement. With ℓ read-out sites and n_s syndromes you need

M = ⌈ n_s / ℓ ⌉ rounds of measurement Fewer read-out sites means less wiring but a deeper syndrome-extraction cycle, and more time for errors to accumulate between corrections.

That single number is the dial between wiring simplicity and fault-tolerant performance, and the paper maps it out: Monte Carlo simulation puts the threshold near 10⁻³, roughly seven orders of magnitude above earlier estimates for globally-controlled arrays that had to protect their auxiliary qubits separately — and it improves systematically as you add measurement sites.

Which is the whole story: a ring, four control channels, no ancillas, a compiler that turns iSWAP into SWAP six pulses at a time, and error correction that finally costs something reasonable.
